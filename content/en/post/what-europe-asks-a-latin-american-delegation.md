## The mistake of arriving with the usual deck

The scene repeats itself. A delegation lands in Vienna, Berlin or Brussels with twenty slides about its city: aerial shots, population, GDP growth, one slide on young talent and another on strategic location. The meeting is scheduled for forty minutes. Ten minutes in, someone on the other side of the table interrupts with a question that was not in the script.

It is not bad faith or disinterest. The European counterpart is not evaluating whether the city is attractive. That is already assumed, otherwise the meeting would not have been accepted. They are evaluating something else: what it costs to operate there and how long it takes to get the money back.

## The four questions that always come up

After more than a decade organizing business missions and institutional visits in Europe, the questions repeat with surprising regularity.

**What does a foreign shareholder actually pay?** Not the headline corporate tax rate, which is in every promotional brochure. What is withheld when profits leave the country, which is the number that really enters the financial model.

**Which companies in the sector already operate there?** A documented presence is worth more than any institutional promise: it means somebody has already solved the problems the newcomer is about to hit, and that a local supplier exists who knows how to do it.

**Where does the investment that already arrives come from?** If most of it comes from a single country, that says a great deal about which treaties are in force and what kind of investor the local bureaucracy is used to handling.

**What has changed in the last five years?** The direction of travel weighs more than the still photograph. A city improving slowly communicates something quite different from one that has spent three years flat on the same indicators.

None of these questions is a secret, and somebody has already answered them in comparable form. Pro Latam publishes [a comparison of thirteen Latin American cities by investment climate](https://invest.pro-latam.org/cities), built with the same analytical structure for all of them, which is useful precisely for anticipating this conversation.

## Know your own profile before your neighbour's

Here is the uncomfortable part. Almost any delegation can recite from memory why its city is special. Very few know how it reads to an outsider who is comparing it against twelve others at the same time.

The exercise is worth doing before boarding the plane: read [the investment profile for Mexico City](https://invest.pro-latam.org/cities/mexico-city) as if you were the European analyst who will consult it the night before the meeting. Which pillars come out strong, which weak, which sectors show a real presence and which appear only as aspiration.

Then read [the equivalent profile for Bogotá](https://invest.pro-latam.org/cities/bogota), or the one for the city you actually compete against. Because the meeting is not won by explaining how good you are. It is won by knowing how you differ from the next name on the list. An expansion director looking at Latin America is not choosing between your city and nothing: they are choosing between your city and three others that fit just as well on paper.

When a delegation arrives having done that work, the register of the conversation changes. You stop selling and start negotiating.

## The agenda is not the trip

The second classic mistake is treating the mission as an accumulation of meetings. Ten appointments in four days looks productive and almost never is: nobody makes an investment decision in an introductory meeting, least of all the fourth one of the day.

What moves things tends to be the opposite. Fewer meetings, better prepared, with the person who actually decides, and with time around them. Decisions are cooked in the conversations that happen before and after the formal appointment, over lunch or on the way there, and those need room in the calendar. A packed agenda signals urgency, and urgency is not a good signal when what is being negotiated is ten-year capital.
